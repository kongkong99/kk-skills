# Interface explainer motion

Turn each spoken idea into a visual state that makes it easier to understand: establish the object, direct attention, show an action or relationship, reveal its result, then move on. Use motion to explain, not to decorate every sentence. When the user requests a close match to a named reference, its typography, composition, transitions, and timing are part of the requested result, not merely inspiration. Inspect the actual reference before authoring. For this user's personal-brand WorkBuddy shorts, use the person-first opening and varied subtitle treatment below; when the user explicitly requests a different reference, follow that selection.

## Scope and inputs

Identify the requested deliverable: analysis/storyboard, editable graphic, timeline edit, or finished video. Gather only inputs needed for that outcome: narration and verified transcript/timing, available screen recordings/screenshots and result states, presenter footage, output format, and any brand or style constraints. Inspect existing project/media first when present. If material is missing, use reasonable assumptions for planning; for production, clearly distinguish a proposed/illustrative interface from verified real operation and ask only for information that blocks the requested result. Do not imply a simulated click, feature, or result actually occurred. By default visibly identify simulations when they could be mistaken for actual operation; if the user explicitly requests no in-video simulation badge, honor that direction and disclose the simulation in delivery without claiming an actual execution or result.

Use existing word-level timing when available; generate or obtain a transcript when needed and check uncertain words/timing. Track source and timeline times separately. If there is no spoken audio yet, mark storyboard timing as provisional rather than pretending to have sync.

## From speech to visual action

1. Break the narration into meaningful beats, not one effect per sentence. For each beat determine: the single thing the audience needs to understand, the visual evidence or state change, the focus, and its corresponding spoken cue.
2. Choose the simplest form that makes the beat legible:

   | Meaning | Visual choice |
   | --- | --- |
   | Establish object or context | Show enough of the original interface to orient the viewer before cropping. |
   | Point to a control | Move attention with a crop/zoom, cursor, outline, or subdued surroundings; preserve the target and context. |
   | Show an operation | Keep the real action readable; use a click/shortcut/input cue when it clarifies an otherwise invisible step. |
   | Show consequence | Hold a verifiable result; highlight the changed area or compare before and after. |
   | Explain a relationship | Reveal only needed cards, nodes, and arrows in the order of the explanation; retain common elements across changes. |
   | Compare alternatives | Keep the shared structure stable; reveal meaningful differences in position, labels, or color. |

3. Choose the appropriate material: real screen recording for accurate operations; real screenshot plus separately recreated moving details where that improves clarity; or an explicitly illustrative UI/diagram for concepts. Match actual interface features and avoid fabricating results. Use the user's own palette and content where supplied.
4. Assign composition according to the beat: presenter plus interface can introduce or conclude; a full-frame screen detail or diagram gives complex information space; a small presenter inset can maintain presence if it does not obstruct. Do not force split-screen throughout. Keep a single clear focal point, legible phone-sized text, uncluttered context, and room for captions/platform controls.
5. Build a beat sheet appropriate to the task with speech cue/time, intended understanding, source/real-or-illustrative status, visible before/after state, layout, focal action, and expected result. Align reveals and actions to actual speech cues and allow enough time to read. Use durations and easing as adjustable starting choices, never as measured facts about the reference.

## Speech cleanup and opening speed

For this user's short videos, set the first three complete spoken sentences of the retained edit to **1.1× playback speed**, with presenter picture and original speech synchronized; resume 1.0× from sentence four. Count semantic sentences, not caption cards, line breaks or isolated ASR fragments. If fewer than three sentences remain, apply it to those available. Do this after speech cleanup and before locking downstream timing, preserving voice pitch when supported. Set the intended 1.1× rate once rather than multiplying it on each revision. Follow [first-three-sentence timing](references/jeff-person-openings.md#前三句口播11倍速); all first-two/first-ten-second rules are measured on the resulting final timeline.

For this user's speech-led finished videos, include removal or reduction of prominent breaths in the A-roll edit before locking timing for captions, visuals and sound effects. Locate audible inhalations using listening and available waveform or other reliable tools. Shorten or cut isolated breaths when doing so preserves the natural rhythm; where a breath overlaps speech, use a short local gain reduction or a gentle fade instead of cutting into a word. Protect word beginnings and endings, intentional pauses and speech meaning; retain a subtle breath when removing it would make the delivery sound unnatural. Keep picture and audio synchronized and re-align captions and effects after any time-changing edits.

Pause compression can help pacing, but it does not establish that audible breaths have been detected or removed; voice isolation or noise reduction alone is not proof of breath removal either. Review the edited transitions and exported audio by listening for residual loud breaths, clipped syllables, clicks, abrupt gaps and synchronization. If reliable breath identification or listening is unavailable, do not guess destructive cuts or claim complete removal: finish the other authorized edits and report what remains unverified.

## Cover and first frame for this user's finished short videos

Treat the cover as a required timeline asset and export gate. Follow [cover selection, placement and verification](references/cover-first-frame.md), in this order:

1. Compare at least three actual video frames at phone size. Record the candidates, selected timestamp and reason. Choose a sharp, naturally expressive, unobstructed subject that represents the topic; do not automatically save the existing frame zero and call it selected.
2. Save the chosen, fully composed cover as a full-resolution PNG, including this user's mandatory topic headline and bottom gradient, without duplicating those layers.
3. Import it and place an enabled, opaque, full-canvas cover image at timeline frame 0 for one frame (or the shortest supported duration), above other picture layers, with no fade. Do this even when the selected image resembles the existing opening. Preserve every audio cue and all other timing; no ripple insertion or silent leader.
4. Read back the placed cover's asset, range, layer and geometry. Export, then run `scripts/check_cover_first_frame.py` against the chosen PNG and the actual exported video's first decoded frame. Resolve mismatches; do not redefine the chosen cover by extracting the incorrect export.
5. Inspect the cover-to-motion handoff and first seconds for flashes, jumps, occlusion and audio delay. Record both timeline-placement evidence and the export comparison report. A separate cover file, a thumbnail or a passing image comparison alone does not prove this workflow was completed. If any check is unavailable, report it as unverified rather than marking the cover done.

The first-frame image does not establish which thumbnail a publishing platform will choose or crop. Do not promise a platform cover unless verified there.

## Execute the requested deliverable

For a storyboard, give enough detail to produce each beat. For an editable animation or finished video, carry the plan through using the available host's editing, screen, caption, motion-graphic and export tools; follow their applicable instructions. Discover and use an available product-specific workflow when the task concerns a supported product or simulator. Do not stop at a plan when the user requested production. Preserve original source files while editing and arranging copies or timeline occurrences within the requested scope. Keep presenter, interface, graphics and captions separately editable where the host permits. Parameterize reusable text, colors, media, layout, and cues when practical.

For a close reference match, implement the actual title, subtitle treatment and shot-level composition as separate editable elements; do not substitute default captions, a fixed header or static split layout. If the host has caption-template-impl or create-motion-graphics workflows, use them where needed and follow their requirements. When authoring graphics, use the host's actual runtime contract. Drive motion by time in seconds, or derive seconds from the timeline frame rate; do not assume a fixed fps. Design the readable settled frame before animating it. A reusable skill guides decisions; it is not itself a renderer or a substitute for unavailable source footage.

The user's mandatory persistent headline takes precedence over the general reference-matching warning against substituting a fixed header. Keep it alongside the requested shot-specific composition and speech captions.

## Verify

Verify the retained first three sentence ranges are 1.1×, their picture and original voice remain synchronized, the fourth sentence returns to 1.0×, and the speed boundary does not clip words. Recheck captions, keyword emphasis, image beats and cues against the retimed speech; preserve the whoosh's required usable duration rather than speeding up the entire composite.

For this user's series, verify uninterrupted headline and bottom-gradient coverage throughout spoken sections and short pauses, including scene changes and the first-frame cover. Inspect conflicts at camera extremes and B-roll/interface/chat cutaways; neither overlay may hide the face or important content.

Check every presenter-inset clip, including its entry, middle and exit: the visible mask must be circular in final output pixels and remain bottom-right. Inspect the composed/exported picture, not only the mask parameters; normalized ellipse radii on a non-square source may need different values to produce a circle.

Inspect the composed output, not just isolated graphic assets: entry, readable hold, operation/result, exit, and the most crowded or zoomed frame. Check each selected keyword before/during/after its spoken cue: unchanged color, one size emphasis and no repeated flashing. Check extra graphics add information and audit the first-ten-second event sheet, including two distinct generated images actually displayed there and their specific spoken meanings. Verify star motion is gentle and continuous. Listen for action-appropriate cue variety, intact whoosh bodies and releases, synchronization, clear voice and no clipping. Check visible targets, honest real/simulated status, speech alignment, final-size text and unobstructed content. For a close match, compare corresponding reference/output beats for layout, type, background, proportions and motion; account for this user's explicit overrides. Fix actual mismatches, recheck affected portions and report unverified aspects. For planning-only work, check the storyboard without claiming a render was tested.
